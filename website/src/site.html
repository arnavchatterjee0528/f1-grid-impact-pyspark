Does qualifying decide the race?

We ran 75 years of Formula 1 results and recorded laps through Apache Spark to measure how much the starting grid decides the finish, where positions change during a race, and how well a result can be predicted before the start.

01The question

Why ask it with Big Data

Every Saturday, F1 teams choose between a car set up for one fast qualifying lap and one set up for a long race. That trade-off only makes sense if you know how much the grid position is worth. Broadcasters, fantasy and prediction platforms, and the people who design circuits ask the same question from different sides.

Four questions

  1. How strongly does grid position predict the finishing position, and has that changed by decade?
  2. Which circuits are the hardest and easiest places to gain or lose positions?
  3. When positions do change, how: on the opening lap, through pit stops, when cars ahead retire, or in normal running?
  4. Can a model predict the finishing position from pre-race information alone, and how does it compare with a published 2025 paper?

The scenario

Domain
Sports analytics: Formula 1
Problem
No scalable, per-circuit measure of how much the grid decides a race, and no reproducible pre-race prediction
Who uses it
Team strategists, broadcasters and analysts, fantasy and prediction platforms, race organisers
Data type
Historical, 1950–2024 seasons. Nothing streamed, nothing simulated.
Why Spark
The answers need 14 tables joined together and window functions over lap records. The same code runs unchanged on telemetry data that is millions of rows per season.
02The data

Fourteen tables, one race at a time

The Formula 1 World Championship dataset on Kaggle is built from the Ergast / Jolpica-F1 database. It stores every race since 1950 as linked CSV files: one row per driver per race in results, one row per driver per lap in lap_times, and lookup tables for drivers, teams, circuits and finish statuses.

How the tables connect

Every analysis starts from results and joins outward on the ID columns.

Big Data characteristics

Volume

About 700,000 rows, including lap records. Small for one machine, but the pipeline is written to scale to telemetry.

Variety

Integers, lap times stored as text, dates and categories across 14 linked tables.

Veracity

Missing values stored as text, retirements, shared drives, no lap data before 1996 and no pit data before 2011.

Velocity

Not applicable. This is historical batch data.

06Finding 2

      
Some tracks hold the grid order

        
The overtaking index is the average number of places a finisher gains or loses between the grid and the flag at that circuit. It covers 2010–2024 and circuits with at least 5 races. Earlier seasons had 30-car grids and frequent breakdowns, which would make old circuits look easy to pass at.

07Finding 3

      
Four ways a position changes

        
A driver's position can change for very different reasons. For 2011–2024, the seasons with both lap and pit-stop data, the pipeline separates them. It labels every lap-to-lap position change as the opening lap, a pit stop, a car ahead retiring, or normal running. These are position-change estimates from timing data, not a verified count of overtakes.

09Finding 4

      
Predicting the result before the start

        
Spark MLlib models trained on 2010–2022 and tested on the full 2023 season, the same split as the paper we compare against. They use pre-race information only: the grid slot, the qualifying gap to pole, the driver's and the team's average finish over their previous five races, and the circuit's overtaking index.

Responsible use

        
The dataset is public on Kaggle, built from the open Ergast / Jolpica database, and used here for academic, non-commercial work. It contains only public race results and professional drivers' names. No credentials are stored, the CSVs are not redistributed with the code, and only measured results are reported.
